A patient arrives for a 10:15 appointment. At check-in, staff realize the conversation needs professional language support. The provider is ready. The question is whether anyone already knows what happens next.

That's worth settling before it comes up. This guide covers what to look for in a medical interpreter, how to evaluate a service, how the modalities differ, and how to build a process staff can follow.

## Quick Answer: What to Look for in a Medical Interpreter

When evaluating a medical interpreter, look for:

- **Appropriate language proficiency**, demonstrated rather than self-reported
- **Interpreter training**, covering the mechanics and ethics of the role
- **Accuracy and impartiality** — no changes, omissions, or additions
- **Familiarity with medical terminology** appropriate to your setting
- **Confidentiality and professional ethics** the interpreter is accountable to
- **Qualification or certification appropriate to the assignment**
- **The modality you need** — audio, video, or onsite

The rest of this article covers how to evaluate each, and what to put in place before a patient is waiting.

## Why Professional Medical Interpretation Matters

Communication accuracy is the practical reason to use trained interpreters, and there's direct research on it. A 2012 study in *Annals of Emergency Medicine* analyzed 57 pediatric emergency department encounters with Spanish-speaking families and found 1,884 interpreter errors, 18% with potential clinical consequences. The proportion varied by interpreter: 12% professional, 22% ad hoc (family members or untrained staff), 20% with none present. Training mattered more than tenure — interpreters with 100+ hours of training averaged a median of 12 errors per encounter versus 33 for those with less.

AHRQ's guide for hospitals adds the patient-safety view: adverse events affecting patients with limited English proficiency "are more frequently caused by communication problems and more likely to result in serious harm compared to English-speaking patients."

A systematic review by Karliner and colleagues in *Health Services Research* found professional interpreters were associated with overall improvement in care for these patients, particularly in communication and in reducing utilization disparities. On clinical outcomes it identified only two studies, with mixed results.

So: research has associated professional interpreter use with improvements in communication and aspects of quality of care, while evidence on some clinical and utilization outcomes remains mixed. Treat interpretation as a communication-quality decision, not a promise of a clinical result.

## Bilingual Does Not Automatically Mean Qualified to Interpret

Nearly every practice has someone who speaks a second language and gets asked to help. They're usually glad to. The issue isn't willingness or ability — speaking two languages and interpreting between them in a clinical encounter are different competencies.

Federal regulation describes the difference. Under 45 CFR 92.4, a qualified interpreter for an individual with limited English proficiency has demonstrated proficiency in both languages, can interpret "effectively, accurately, and impartially" using specialized vocabulary "without changes, omissions, or additions," and adheres to generally accepted interpreter ethics principles, including client confidentiality.

Set that against what's asked of a bilingual medical assistant pulled off another task: clinical vocabulary in two languages, no summarizing or softening, neutrality when the news is hard, and a confidentiality standard they can be held to. AHRQ observes that staff who interpret on an ad hoc basis "are more likely to make clinically significant mistakes than qualified medical interpreters." Note that qualified bilingual staff are themselves a recognized part of language assistance services — the distinction isn't between interpreters and bilingual employees, but between people prepared for the role and people handed it unexpectedly.

Family members raise related considerations. AHRQ notes they "typically may not understand the subtle nuances of language and culture," may not question terminology they don't understand, and that confidentiality concerns "may prevent patients from disclosing critical health information."

Federal rules address this with nuance worth preserving. Under 45 CFR 92.201, covered entities generally must not rely on an unqualified adult to interpret — with exceptions including an emergency involving an imminent threat when no qualified interpreter is immediately available, and cases where the patient specifically requests it in private with a qualified interpreter present, the accompanying adult agrees, and the request is documented. Reliance on a minor child is similarly restricted, with a narrow emergency exception.

## Qualified vs. Certified Medical Interpreters

These terms get used interchangeably. They aren't the same thing.

**Qualified** generally relates to demonstrated ability to interpret effectively, accurately, and impartially in the relevant setting, along with adherence to professional ethics. It's a functional standard.

**Certified** means holding a recognized interpreter certification or credential, typically earned by passing a standardized examination.

Certification is one way to demonstrate qualification, and some settings, payers, or internal policies call for it. But not every qualified interpreter holds a national certification, and it does not follow that every healthcare encounter legally requires a certified one. What the standard does rule out is treating "someone who speaks the language" as sufficient. For what applies to your programs, funding sources, and state requirements, ask your compliance team or counsel.

## What Should Your Practice Look for When Choosing an Interpreter Service?

Once you know what a qualified interpreter looks like, the next question is what to ask a service:

- **Interpreter qualifications** — how the service screens and trains, and whether it can tell you the qualifications of the person on a given assignment
- **Available languages**, including the ones your patients need rather than a headline list
- **Audio, video, and onsite options**, so you aren't locked into one modality
- **Scheduled and more immediate needs** — most practices need both; some services handle only one well
- **How staff requests support** — how many steps, and whether a coordinator can do it unaided
- **Backup availability** when an assigned interpreter falls through
- **Ease of use**, which decides whether staff use the service or work around it
- **Confidentiality and security**, including how patient information is handled
- **Administrative workflow** — scheduling, records, and billing

## Phone, Video or Onsite Medical Interpretation?

There is no universally correct choice. The appropriate modality depends on the nature of the encounter, the individual's communication needs, available technology, interpreter availability, and the organization's language-access procedures.

If you are weighing remote formats specifically, our guide to [VRI vs OPI](/insights/vri-vs-opi-choosing-the-right-format/) compares them in more depth.

### Audio

Audio can work well when visual communication isn't necessary to the exchange and remote interpretation is appropriate for the encounter — routine scheduling conversations, check-in questions, follow-up calls, medication questions. Nobody can see anything, so it's less suited to exchanges that depend on gesture, demonstration, or visual context.

### Video

Video adds visual information: facial expression, gesture, and the ability to see what a provider is demonstrating.

Sign language interpretation deserves specific care. Video remote interpreting can be useful, and ADA guidance notes it "can be especially useful in rural areas where on-site interpreters may be difficult to obtain." That same guidance is explicit that "VRI will not be effective in all circumstances" — for example, when someone has difficulty seeing the screen because of vision loss, injury, or positioning — and that "an on-site interpreter may be required." It also directs title II entities to give primary consideration to the aid or service the person requests. Ask, rather than assuming video is the answer.

If you use video remote interpreting, connection quality is a substantive requirement. Federal rules for covered entities specify real-time, full-motion video and audio without lags or choppy, blurry, or grainy images.

### Onsite

An interpreter physically present may be appropriate for longer or more complex encounters, situations with several participants, and cases where a patient's communication needs make remote interpretation impractical. The tradeoff is scheduling, since onsite generally requires advance arrangement.

## What Happens When Your Usual Interpreter Isn't Available?

This is where language-access plans tend to fall apart. An arrangement works until the interpreter cancels, a walk-in arrives, or a language comes up nobody planned for. A workable fallback answers four questions in advance:

- **Who does staff contact** when the primary arrangement doesn't hold?
- **What alternative methods** are available, and which suit which encounters?
- **How does staff access the backup** — the actual steps, not the vendor name?
- **Who has authority to request it**, so nobody waits for approval that isn't required?

Write the answers down. A fallback that lives in one person's head isn't one.

## Build Your Language-Access Plan Before You Need It

Answer these seven questions in advance and write the answers where staff can find them.

1. **How does staff identify when professional interpretation may be needed?** AHRQ recommends collecting language preference systematically at registration, early in the care process, rather than discovering it at the front desk.
2. **Who is responsible for requesting an interpreter?** Name the role, not the person.
3. **What interpretation options are available?** Audio, video, onsite, and which encounters each suits.
4. **What happens when the usual interpreter isn't available?** The fallback, written down.
5. **How does staff access the service?** The steps, specific enough for someone in their first week.
6. **How are scheduled and more immediate needs handled?** Different workflows; both need one.
7. **How does the organization determine appropriate interpreter qualifications?** Which encounters call for what, and who decides.

Take these to a staff meeting before the next language-access need arises. The discussion surfaces most of the gaps on its own.

## Final Takeaway

Finding a medical interpreter shouldn't be something staff work out while a patient waits. It belongs in an established communication process — decided in advance, documented, and known to the people who need it at the front desk and in the exam room.

The practices that handle this well aren't the ones with the most resources. They're the ones that answered the questions first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I find a medical interpreter for my practice?

Start by defining what your practice needs: which languages come up, whether requests are typically scheduled or same-day, and which encounters call for audio, video, or an onsite interpreter. Then evaluate interpreter services against those needs. Look for demonstrated language proficiency, interpreter training, the ability to interpret accurately and impartially, familiarity with medical terminology, accountability to confidentiality and professional ethics, and qualifications appropriate to the assignment.

### Can bilingual staff interpret for patients?

Qualified bilingual staff are a recognized part of language assistance services. The concern is with staff who are bilingual but haven't been prepared for the interpreting role. AHRQ notes that hospital staff who interpret on an ad hoc basis are more likely to make clinically significant mistakes than qualified medical interpreters. Speaking two languages and interpreting accurately and impartially in a clinical encounter are different competencies.

### Can a family member interpret?

Federal rules restrict this rather than banning it outright. Under 45 CFR 92.201, covered entities generally must not rely on an unqualified adult to interpret — with exceptions including an emergency involving an imminent threat when no qualified interpreter is immediately available, and cases where the patient specifically requests it in private with a qualified interpreter present, the accompanying adult agrees, and the request is documented. Reliance on a minor child is similarly restricted with a narrow emergency exception. AHRQ also notes practical concerns about nuance, terminology, and confidentiality.

### What is the difference between a qualified and a certified medical interpreter?

Qualified generally relates to demonstrated ability to interpret effectively, accurately, and impartially in the relevant setting, along with adherence to professional ethics. Certified means holding a recognized interpreter certification, usually earned by examination. Certification is one way to demonstrate qualification, and some settings or policies call for it — but not every qualified interpreter is certified, and it does not follow that every healthcare encounter legally requires a certified interpreter.

### Should my practice use phone, video, or onsite interpretation?

It depends on the encounter. The appropriate modality depends on the nature of the encounter, the individual's communication needs, available technology, interpreter availability, and your organization's language-access procedures. Audio can suit exchanges where visual communication isn't necessary. Video adds visual information. Onsite may fit longer or more complex encounters. For sign language specifically, ADA guidance notes video remote interpreting will not be effective in all circumstances and that an onsite interpreter may be required, and directs title II entities to give primary consideration to the individual's expressed choice.

### What should we do if our scheduled interpreter is unavailable?

Decide in advance. A workable fallback names who staff contacts, what alternative methods are available, the actual steps to access the backup, and who has authority to request the service without waiting for approval. Document it, because a fallback that exists only in one person's memory isn't one.
