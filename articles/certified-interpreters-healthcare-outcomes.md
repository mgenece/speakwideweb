# How Certified Interpreters Are Changing Healthcare Outcomes for LEP Patients

Over 68 million people in the United States speak a language other than English at home. For the estimated 25 million with limited English proficiency (LEP), a routine doctor's visit can become a dangerous guessing game — unless a certified interpreter is present.

The evidence is clear: professional interpreters save lives, reduce errors, and lower costs. Here's what the research shows and what it means for your healthcare organization.

## The Problem: Language Barriers Cost Lives

When LEP patients can't communicate effectively with their providers, the consequences are severe:

- **Misdiagnosis** from incomplete symptom descriptions
- **Medication errors** from unclear dosage instructions
- **Delayed care** due to inability to schedule or navigate the system
- **Lower follow-through** on treatment plans
- **Higher readmission rates** and longer hospital stays

A landmark study published in the Journal of General Internal Medicine found that LEP patients are twice as likely to experience adverse events during hospital stays compared to English-speaking patients — and those events are more likely to cause harm.

## What Certified Interpreters Change

### Accuracy of Clinical Communication

Professional medical interpreters are trained not just in language, but in medical terminology, ethics, and communication techniques. They catch nuances — a patient who says "my stomach hurts a little" might mean severe pain in their cultural context. A certified interpreter understands this. A bilingual receptionist does not.

### Informed Consent

Informed consent requires that patients truly understand their diagnosis, treatment options, and risks. Using a family member or untrained staff member to interpret during a consent conversation is a legal and ethical minefield — and a common source of malpractice claims.

### Medication Adherence

Patients who understand their treatment plans follow them. Studies show LEP patients with professional interpretation have significantly higher medication adherence rates than those relying on ad-hoc interpretation.

### Patient Satisfaction

Press Ganey data consistently shows LEP patients report higher satisfaction scores when professional interpreters are used. This matters for HCAHPS ratings, CMS reimbursement, and institutional reputation.

## The Regulatory Layer

Healthcare organizations serving LEP patients have clear legal obligations:

**Title VI** requires any federally funded program — including Medicaid and Medicare providers — to provide free language access to LEP individuals.

**Section 1557 of the ACA** explicitly prohibits discrimination based on national origin (which includes language) in health programs receiving federal assistance.

**HIPAA** requires effective communication with all patients, including LEP individuals.

The Department of Health and Human Services Office for Civil Rights (OCR) actively investigates complaints. Hospitals and FQHCs that cannot demonstrate language access compliance face funding consequences.

## The "Family Member" Problem

Many providers default to using a patient's family member as an interpreter — it's free, it's fast, and it feels natural. But it creates serious problems:

- Family members may soften or change what the patient says
- Patients may withhold information to protect family members from distress
- Children used as interpreters experience psychological harm
- Confidentiality is compromised
- Legal liability for the provider increases

HHS guidance specifically discourages the use of family members as interpreters except in emergency situations where no qualified interpreter is available.

## What FQHCs and Private Practices Can Do

1. **Audit your LEP patient population** — know which languages your patients speak
2. **Establish a language access plan** — required for Title VI compliance
3. **Integrate certified interpretation** into your booking and intake workflow
4. **Document every interpretation session** — interpreter name, certification, duration, language pair
5. **Train clinical staff** on how to work effectively with interpreters
