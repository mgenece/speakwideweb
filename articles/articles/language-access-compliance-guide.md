# Language Access Compliance: What Every Business Needs to Know

If your organization serves the public — whether as a law firm, healthcare clinic, construction company, or government contractor — you likely have legal obligations to provide language access to Limited English Proficient (LEP) individuals. Failing to meet those obligations can result in federal penalties, loss of funding, and civil liability.

Here's what you need to know.

## What Is Language Access?

Language access means that individuals who do not speak English fluently have meaningful access to your services through certified interpretation and translation. This isn't optional goodwill — it is a federally mandated requirement for many industries.

## The Key Laws

**Title VI of the Civil Rights Act (1964)**
Any entity receiving federal financial assistance — including hospitals, FQHCs, legal aid organizations, and public contractors — must provide language access to LEP individuals at no cost to the client.

**Executive Order 13166 (2000)**
Requires all federal agencies and federally funded programs to provide meaningful access to LEP persons. This order directly impacts Medicaid, Medicare, CHIP, and federally funded legal services.

**HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)**
Under HIPAA, healthcare providers must ensure effective communication with all patients, including those with limited English proficiency. Using a patient's family member as an interpreter is strongly discouraged and in some cases prohibited.

**OSHA Hazard Communication Standard**
OSHA requires safety information and training to be provided in a language workers actually understand. Multilingual safety training is mandatory in construction, manufacturing, and logistics.

## What Counts as a "Qualified Interpreter"?

Not just anyone fluent in a language qualifies. A certified interpreter must:
- Demonstrate language proficiency in both languages
- Have specialized knowledge in the relevant field (medical, legal, or workplace)
- Adhere to a professional code of ethics (confidentiality, impartiality, accuracy)
- Hold recognized credentials (e.g., court certification, CMI, CHI)

Bilingual staff members, family members, and children should **never** serve as interpreters in regulated settings.

## The Documentation Requirement

Compliance isn't just about having an interpreter present — it's about proving it. Every interpretation session should generate a log that includes:
- Date and time of the session
- Language pair used
- Name and certification of the interpreter
- Duration of the session
- Client confirmation

## What Happens If You Don't Comply?

- **Loss of federal funding** (hospitals, clinics, legal aid organizations)
- **OCR complaints and investigations** from the HHS Office for Civil Rights
- **Civil lawsuits** from LEP individuals denied services
- **OSHA citations and fines** for workplace safety violations
